# Photorealistic Macro Prompt Guide

## Image prompt formula
[SUBJECT + species/material if known] + [plausible microtexture and droplets] + [environment] + [professional studio/natural lighting direction] + [lens magnification and shallow depth of field] + [palette and negative space] + [16:9 cinematic composition] + [photorealism constraints].

Example: “Extreme photorealistic macro photograph of a pale violet iris petal with fine veining and tiny realistic dew beads, emerging from seamless black velvet shadow; sculpted studio side light from upper left, delicate rim light, shallow focal plane crossing one droplet, tack-sharp surface microtexture, creamy optical bokeh, understated cinematic grading, negative space toward the right, 16:9, no lettering, no artificial geometry.”

## Video prompt formula: one master image, chained last frames
Use one generated master still for the first clip. For every later clip, select the immediately preceding clip's actual saved final frame as its supplied reference; do not generate a separate scene still. Each video prompt follows the same copy-ready order: premium macro scene description, **ENTRY TRANSITION (0–2 seconds)**, **MAIN ACTION (2–8 seconds)**, **EXIT TRANSITION (8–10 seconds)**, and negative constraints.

The first prompt begins from the approved master still. Each next prompt begins by holding the exact composition of the previous saved last frame. The exit must finish on a clean, stable, detailed frame worth saving and using as the next starting image.

## Subject lock and realistic changes
Preserve the principal subject's species/material, identity, anatomy, proportions and physical texture. Do not lock its screen position or require the entire subject to remain visible: extreme push-ins, creative crops, partial visibility and natural exits from frame are valid macro compositions. Limit subject movement to plausible natural motion (for example, a subtle petal tremor or a droplet refracting light). Do not deform, stretch, duplicate, melt, or morph the subject. A new species or color may emerge through a gradual rack focus, moving light, realistic refraction/caustics, soft optical bokeh, or a plausible shadow/occlusion. Show the new subject as a separate object; do not turn the old subject into it.

## In-clip transition effects
Choose restrained effects grounded in professional macro photography:
- A controlled rim-light or exposure sweep that slowly reveals the surface.
- A slow rack focus with stable geometry and lens behavior.
- Optical bokeh or lens bloom that grows or resolves gently.
- Realistic refracted highlights or caustic light through water/petal tissue.
- A gradual shift in white balance or rim-light color.
- A soft foreground shadow or out-of-focus highlight passing across frame.

The effect must begin at the clip opening or finish at its ending, remain photorealistic, and visually pair with the adjacent clip. Avoid hard cuts, black slates, abrupt flashes, glitch effects, or added editor transitions.

## Copy-ready prompt skeleton
“Premium extreme macro [botanical/material] cinematography. [Describe the subject, black background, physical microtexture, studio light, palette and macro optics.]
**ENTRY TRANSITION, 0–2 seconds:** Begin with the exact composition of the provided [master image / previous clip's saved last frame]. Hold the subject geometry, positions, texture and lighting completely stable for a brief moment. Start a very slow, smooth cinematic [camera move].
**MAIN ACTION, 2–8 seconds:** [Choose a distinct macro angle/perspective and one precise continuous camera move—extreme push-in, lateral track, orbit, tilt, or pull-back—plus plausible focus/light effects.] Keep the principal subject anatomically coherent and recognizable through the details shown; it may fill the frame, be cropped by the frame edge, or move out of view during the camera move. [If the next subject or color must emerge, reveal it gradually through realistic focus, refraction, light, bokeh or shadow; show it as a distinct subject, never a transformation.]
**EXIT TRANSITION, 8–10 seconds:** Finish on [precise framing, focus, composition and cue for the next clip]. Lock camera movement and hold a clean, stable, detailed frame suitable for direct use as the starting image of the next clip. [For the final clip, end on the intended closing composition.]
[Negative constraints: no morphing, deformation, duplication, flicker, camera shake, artificial glitter, text, watermark. Do not forbid intended macro crops or partial exits from frame.]”

## Quality checklist
- Each clip visibly begins and ends with its own transition effect.
- The main subject's identity and anatomy remain coherent; framing, scale, angle and visible area may change intentionally.
- Any subject/color change is gradual, physically plausible, and never a morph.
- Entry and exit effects pair across the boundary without an editor-side transition.
- Effects do not add time beyond the generated clip.
- Droplets obey gravity/surface tension and move only with a plausible cause.
- No jitter, flicker, artificial plastic texture, camera jump, or duplicate anatomy.
- Focus and camera movement stay slow and intentional.
- The actual final frame is saved and checked against the next entry cue.

## Creative macro framing

Macro coverage should vary on purpose: alternate oblique grazing angles, low and high petal-level views, lateral parallax, shallow-focus detail, dramatic push-ins, pull-backs and compositions where only part of the subject is visible. Let a petal edge, stamen, droplet or flower center fill the frame or pass beyond an edge when the move motivates it. Subjects may leave frame; preserve anatomical coherence in visible detail and use focus, light, texture or motion direction to make the next chained entry feel connected. Shape light deliberately with raking side light, rim light, controlled caustics, and warm/cool shifts. Never use framing rules such as fixed percentage occupancy or full-frame margins as universal constraints.
